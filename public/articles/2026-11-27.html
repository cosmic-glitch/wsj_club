How will Anthropic’s cashed-up employees spend their riches?

Charities are headed for a windfall

THIS THANKSGIVING, the 5,000-odd people who toil at Anthropic have more to be grateful for than most. The artificial-intelligence lab’s shares may have slid below their offer price since Anthropic’s record-breaking flotation on November 19th, but by The Economist’s calculations their combined holdings are still worth a tidy $470bn. What will they do with it all?

Give it away, largely. When the company was born, just six years ago, its seven founders set aside 80% of their roughly equal stakes for charity, something startup bosses usually think about only when their companies are sold or listed. Together they have pledged shares now worth more than $110bn, in excess of Facebook’s entire market value when it was listed in 2012.

There is more. About half of Anthropic’s employees—or “Ants”—have pledged shares of their own; topped up by the company’s donation-matching scheme, this adds almost another $100bn. Add the 5% of shares promised to Anthropic’s charity-oriented kitty, the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, and the company estimates that around 20% of its stock will be “dedicated to the public good” (see chart).

Even if this takes years to materialise, or some evaporates, it is by all accounts an unprecedented sum heading charities’ way. Jack Lewars, founder of Ultra Philanthropy, a firm that advises donors, calls it a “funding Anthropalypse” (more benign than it sounds) that could permanently alter the nature of charitable giving. Charities favoured by effective altruists, a well-meaning if sometimes overzealous group commonly found in the AI world, are likely to benefit handsomely.

Dario Amodei, Tom Brown and Jack Clark, three of Anthropic’s founders, have signed the Giving What We Can (GWWC) pledge, promising to donate 10% of their lifetime income—about $6bn altogether, if they count their shares as such—to “whichever organisations can most effectively use it to improve the lives of others”. GWWC recommends donating to charities working on malaria prevention, vitamin A supplements for children, vaccinations, animal welfare and preventing global catastrophic risks (including from rogue AI). Sjir Hoeijmakers, GWWC’s chief executive, says at least a dozen Anthropic employees have spoken publicly about signing the pledge. Far more have probably signed without advertising the fact.

Ants will have plenty left over after their donations: about $260bn, we calculate. They may not know what to do with it. “People at Anthropic are super smart,” says Michael Arlein, a family-finance lawyer at Patterson Belknap, “but they really don’t have a lot of experience with money.” Young and spouseless, they are setting up trusts in their parents’ or siblings’ names to fund retirement plans or pay off student loans. Many may dive into San Francisco’s ultra-hot housing market, where there is chatter about a “mansion shortage”.

That apart, conventional displays of wealth are unlikely to appeal. In Anthropic’s early days, one founder was discomfited when a sushi chef stopped by the office to prepare an all-you-can-eat spread, a former employee reported: “tbh I’d like it better if we just served rice and beans,” the founder wrote on Slack. Ants’ spending tastes tend to the science-fictional. Amanda Askell, the firm’s resident philosopher, said she coveted the rocket-fitted Tesla Roadster that Elon Musk demonstrated last month. Mr Clark once wished for an AI-powered robotic exoskeleton to help with back spasms.

For financial counsel, many Ants are leaning on their most trusted adviser: the chatbot Claude. And after the recent slide in Anthropic’s share price, they may look to diversify their investments. Some of their money is likely to land at startups being founded by employees looking for a new challenge, reckons Harrison Rolfes, an analyst at PitchBook, a data provider. Let the new Ant colonies—and all those charities—bloom. ■
