Tiny, Endangered Raccoons Learn to Make Toys

Cozumel, Mexico —

A group of small endangered raccoons only found on an island in Mexico have shocked scientists by creating their own toy balls. The clever creatures even seem to have learned from each other how to make the toys.

Pygmy raccoons are a much smaller version of the larger, more commonly known raccoons. They weigh just 6 to 9 pounds (2.7 to 4 kilograms), far less than most raccoons, which can weigh 40 pounds (18 kilograms) or more.

These tiny raccoons are seriously endangered. Fewer than 200 of them are still alive. They are only found on Cozumel Island in Mexico, where they live in the mangroves along the coast.

In the past, the raccoons hunted crabs and snails for food. But Cozumel is a popular tourist spot. The food scraps tourists throw in the trash have become a favorite source of meals for the raccoons. Even though the food – including tortilla chips – isn’t healthy for the animals, they spend a lot of time going through garbage cans.

Nessie O’Neil is a scientist who studies pygmy raccoons. Last January, she was surprised to see one young female raccoon ignore the food in the trash can and instead pull out a paper receipt. The raccoon took the receipt, got it wet, and then rolled it on the sand until it formed a ball.

“Then,” Ms. O’Neil said, “She put it on the ground and was just batting it back and forth.” She had made a toy. Ms. O’Neil continued to study this raccoon and others nearby for 64 hours, spread over eight days.

The first raccoon’s sister saw the toy ball, and tried to make one herself. But she struggled. At first, she had the wrong kinds of paper. But when she couldn’t make a ball with the right kind of paper, the first raccoon decided to take over. She took the paper and rolled it into a ball, while her sister played with the ball she’d already made.

In time, the sister figured out how to make her own ball. Even the mother of the two raccoons tried to make a ball. During the time that Ms. O’Neil watched, the raccoons tried to make balls 14 times. Eight of those efforts were successful.

The raccoons seemed to enjoy batting the balls around. Once, the two sisters played with the ball along with two similar animals called coatis.

A number of animals have been known to make or use tools. But very few animals are known to create toys. This is the first reported case of raccoons making toys.

The raccoons’ behavior shows that they’re even more intelligent than scientists previously thought. It also raises some interesting questions.

Do the toy balls suggest that the raccoons have a “culture”? In science, culture means animals learn new behaviors from each other instead of just being born with them. Scientists want to know if this skill will be passed down to other pygmy raccoons in the future.

That leads to a bigger, more important question: can these endangered animals be protected so that they actually have a future?
