British Girl, 11, Is World’s Youngest Woman Grandmaster

In late September at a huge international chess tournament in Uzbekistan, 11-year-old Bodhana Sivanandan of England made history. Her strong performance in the contest led to her becoming the youngest person ever to earn the chess title of “woman grandmaster”.

Chess & Rating Strong Players Chess is a game that’s easy to learn. But it takes lots of study, practice, and time spent playing to become really good at it. Strong players are given number ratings by FIDE (the International Chess Federation) based on who they’ve played and beaten. To earn titles like “international master” or “grandmaster”, players must also earn “norms” – official tournament performances that prove a player can beat strong opponents. Woman grandmaster is a special title that can only be earned by females.

Bodhana lives in Harrow, a town in northwest London. She didn’t start out to become a chess wizard. She started playing in 2020, during the Covid lockdown – almost by accident. A friend had given her family some toys and books. Among these items, Bodhana found a chess set. She loved the pieces and wanted to play with them like little toys. But her father explained that there was also a game she could play with them.

Bodhana didn’t have a chess teacher. So she learned by watching videos on YouTube. Soon, she was better than her father. Both of her parents are trained as engineers, but neither is very good at playing chess.

Even so, Bodhana’s skills grew quickly. In July, 2021, she took part in a tournament for the first time at the Harrow Chess Club. At age seven, she won every single game she played at the European Schools Championship.

In 2024, she was chosen for England’s team in the Chess Olympiad. She was only nine. That made her the youngest person to represent England in any sport. Last year, she became the youngest female to defeat a grandmaster. In August, she became the Women’s British Chess Champion.

This year, Bodhana was again on England’s team for the Chess Olympiad. But this time, she played on board one – the spot saved for the country’s best player. The competition took place in Samarkand, Uzbekistan.

Going into the tournament, Bodhana already had a rating of over 2,300 and had two of the three norms she needed to become a woman grandmaster. At the Olympiad, she won six games, drew (tied) four, and lost just one. Her strong showing earned her the third norm, and made her the world’s youngest woman grandmaster ever.

Despite her successes, Bodhana is still just a kid. She has to balance her chess life with school and other interests like reading and playing the piano and violin. And when she does play chess, she uses a booster seat so she can see and reach the board easily. When asked if she felt nervous about the big games, Bodhana said, “I just tried to enjoy it.”

Bodhana is proud of what she’s done, but she isn’t planning on stopping there. She’s working to become an international master (IM) – a higher rank that requires a rating of 2,400 and more norms. After the Olympiad, Bodhana’s rating is 2,382, and she has two of the three norms she needs for the IM title.

After that would come the challenge of becoming a grandmaster. “I want to keep going,” she told FIDE, “And keep making history.”
