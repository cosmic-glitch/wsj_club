Donald Trump should love the new documentary about Elizabeth Holmes

But when will other Americans get tired of hearing that truth is a slippery thing?

“What is the reality?” asks the disgraced entrepreneur Elizabeth Holmes near the start of Nathan Fielder’s new documentary, “You Can See Everything”, about the 34 days he spent with her before she reported to serve a prison sentence for fraud in 2023. And the audience member thinks, Oh no, here we go again.

Or, at least, so thinks that audience member who has grown weary of this endless post-modern era, of being reminded that objective truth is elusive, that one’s purchase on reality is determined by whichever narrative one chooses to believe. By the end of this ingenious, absorbing film, Mr Fielder and his co-director, Lance Oppenheim, have not only questioned whether Ms Holmes is truly guilty of defrauding investors by claiming, as the founder of the blood-testing company Theranos, to have revolutionised the detection of dozens of illnesses. They have raised doubts about the motives of her romantic partner, the integrity of the New York Times and even their own portrayal of Ms Holmes. You can’t see everything. So how can you trust anything?

Americans absorbed this lesson long ago. They might not all have read Michel Foucault on how power shapes truth, or Jean Baudrillard on how modern media erase the boundaries between authenticity and artifice. (Lexington hasn’t got around to it, either.) But they have watched plenty of reality television and become sophisticated enough about the plasticity of truth to have elected their first post-modern president, not once but twice. Or is it three times? Narratives differ.

It is paradoxical, though characteristic of the self-sabotaging academy, that an intellectual movement meant to subvert traditional power structures has helped a right-wing American politician magnify the power of the presidency. But from his days lying about the number of storeys in his buildings to his success as a reality-television star to his years in the Oval Office, Donald Trump has exhibited an intuitive mastery of this corrosive post-Enlightenment realism about reality. His basic rule of business and politics has been to trust nothing and no one. “I’m a non-trusting person,” he told CBS News back in 1990.

Mr Trump has succeeded partly by relying on radical scepticism of facts proclaimed not just by other politicians but by the news media, the pollsters, the courts and the scientists. He knows that reflexive cynicism is trailed everywhere by its idiot twin, gaping gullibility about conspiracy theories with only the flimsiest connection to any fact. Election deniers, climate-change deniers and anti-vaxxers are flourishing in America’s years of epistemic crisis. Even the flat-earthers are having a bit of a moment, if the polls are to be believed, which of course they’re not.

To be fair, Ms Holmes, for one, has reason to be annoyed, or jaded, about the nature of narrative, at least as it is constructed by the news media. She was widely celebrated, including in rigorously fact-checked publications, before she was widely demonised. The head-snapping reversal came after reporting by the Wall Street Journal revealed that the seemingly miraculous “Edison” device developed by Theranos was not able to perform the many tests Ms Holmes claimed, and she was eventually found guilty.

It is on this point that Mr Fielder’s documentary is a bit too clever for anyone’s good, except perhaps Ms Holmes’s. She comes across as a nice lady, if a bit strange. One would need a heart of stone not to feel sorry for her and her family as she leaves her toddler and infant behind with her partner, Billy Evans, to begin serving more than 11 years for bilking the likes of Rupert Murdoch and the heirs to the Walmart fortune. Mr Fielder asks penetrating questions, and he is a master of the awkward silence. But Ms Holmes proves his equal, meeting his stillness with stillness and his hooded stare with wide, seemingly guileless blue eyes that at times mist with tears. She believes she has never told a lie, she says, and (unlike the president) she is a trusting person who is “always being real”. Mr Fielder lingers over the possibility that her “fraud” hinged on an epic miscommunication. When she claimed the Edison device “can” work, she meant it could work, once science caught up with it. She did not intend to deceive anyone. “She just thinks differently,” explains Mr Evans. Ms Holmes laments that “the world believes in a reality about me that isn’t true.”

Hyperreality bites

The post-modernists are right. Power does shape the perception of truth and narrative can mould reality. Ms Holmes’s ascent demonstrated that. And, as Mr Trump has claimed, even the courts can be rigged, and juries can deliver mistaken verdicts. Yet the jury in Ms Holmes’s case does not seem to have decided blithely.

After a trial of almost four months, the jury acquitted her on four counts and deadlocked on three, while convicting her on four. It sifted through a mountain of witness testimony and documentary evidence, including that Ms Holmes, as she admitted, personally affixed the logos of pharmaceutical companies to favourable reports on Edison, even though they had reached no such conclusions. To gesture, on the basis of word games, at the idea the jury might have got it wrong is not, as some movie critics have put it, a “bold, perhaps impossible, quest for truth” or a “battle for the essence of truth”. It is, instead, a shrug at the impossibility of apprehending the truth—an act not of idealistic struggle but of surrender, if not nihilism.

Maybe it is silly to get wound up about this. The movie is thought-provoking and funny. But America is about to have another election. If it does not go his way Mr Trump can be expected to reject the results, and maybe even to try to reverse them. He has done that before. Will Americans follow the model of Mr Fielder and so many others, and throw up their hands, uncertain of what the facts truly are? The world may be about to find out. ■
