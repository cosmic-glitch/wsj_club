The global south’s smartphone king goes big on AI

Faced with stiffening competition and rising costs, Transsion plans its next act

On the streets of Nairobi, smartphones branded Tecno, Infinix or itel are a common sight. The sleek devices are all made by Transsion, a Chinese company that is the biggest phone-seller in the global south by volume. Last year it shifted 170m handsets, roughly two-thirds as many as Apple. In Africa more than half of phones purchased are Transsion devices.

The company has thrived by making phones at scale, tailored in price and design to its chosen markets. But with competition intensifying and the artificial-intelligence boom pushing up the cost of semiconductors, it must do more. So it is venturing into new products—and making its own bet on AI.

Transsion’s success has rested on three pillars. First, unlike many of its Chinese rivals, it went global early. Transsion’s founder first started doing business in Africa in the 2000s, and when he set up his current company in 2013 the continent was its focus. A few years later it expanded into India and South-East Asia. Today it sells handsets in over 100 countries through a vast web of 2,900 distributors, helped by ties with local telecoms providers.

Second is the tailoring. Transsion’s handsets typically cost less than a tenth as much as an iPhone, making them attractive to customers with little money to spend on a device. They are also designed to suit local needs. For Africa, where power outages are frequent, Transsion has focused on long battery life and speedy charging. It has also developed “sweat-resistant” USB ports that better cope with the hot climate. For gaming-mad South-East Asia, it has developed cooling cases, sold as a separate accessory. (The company’s built-in gaming app has 140m monthly users.)

Third is scale. Transsion makes about 40% of its smartphones in six factories of its own (four in China, one each in Bangladesh and Ethiopia) and outsources the rest to 17 contractors, mostly in China. Thanks in part to this hybrid set-up, the phone-maker has proved nimble, taking four to eight months to develop its smartphones.

The strategy had been working well. Revenue grew by 170% from 2019, the year it listed its shares in Shanghai, to 2024 (see chart). But since then it has stagnated. Since April 2024 the firm’s share price has fallen by roughly half.

In its newer markets, Transsion has encountered stiffer competition, including from global giants. In Brazil, Latin America’s biggest smartphone market, which Transsion entered in 2021, Samsung is pushing fancier devices with agentic AI. In the first half of 2026 the South Korean firm’s shipments to the country were 10% higher than a year earlier, while those of Infinix, Transsion’s main brand in Brazil, fell by 24%, according to Counterpoint Research, a firm of analysts.

Rising chip costs—a problem across the industry—are also biting. Memory chips accounted for nearly half the cost of making Transsion’s products in the first four months of the year, up from a fifth in 2023. The share has probably risen further since then. As a result, the company has jacked up handset prices, which has shielded its gross margin but caused a decline in the volume of smartphone sales in the first half of the year. Users are holding on to older devices for longer.

In response to these twin problems, Transsion is branching out. It is now selling customers not only smart watches, earbuds and kitchen appliances, but also energy-storage products, including portable battery modules, and even two- and three-wheeler electric vehicles. That may sound like a stretch, but in the global south consumers often stick to familiar brands, and it helps that Transsion’s operating systems are integrated across its products. Sales of products other than handsets made up 16% of revenue in the first half of 2026, double the share two years earlier.

But Transsion’s biggest long-term bet is on AI. Last month it raised $390m in a Hong Kong stock-market listing; it plans to use two-fifths of that to work on AI models and applications. Already some of its devices incorporate digital assistants and other AI features. The business is also collaborating with Chinese research institutions to work on things such as visual foundation models and voice recognition. A fifth of its employees are engaged in research and development. As Transsion transitions to AI, there will be plenty for them to do. ■
