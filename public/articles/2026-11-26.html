Humanoid robots are coming—sooner than you think

Their first destination may be the home

“RING THE doorbell and hand the delivery to Reflex,” reads the sign on the door of an industrial unit in Brooklyn. Couriers could be forgiven for dropping their parcels and fleeing when they see Reflex, the name both of the company that occupies the unit and its main product. A two-metre-tall humanoid robot with multi-jointed arms, pincers for hands and a wheeled pedestal where its legs should be, it is not your typical receptionist.

Reflex does more than just open doors. It is an all-purpose dogsbody, able to carry out a vast array of physical tasks, albeit with an emphasis on the heavy and repetitive over the dexterous and artisanal. The firm’s promotional material shows the robot assembling cardboard boxes, chucking rubbish in the bin, scanning labels on packages and shovelling snow. Most of the units in the facility in Brooklyn endlessly load and unload pallets, gathering data for the firm’s machine-learning models.

General purpose robots—usually, but not always, humanoid in form—are the pinnacle of a certain sort of vision of the future: artificial general intelligence, freed from the screen and released into the real world. The prospect is tantalisingly close. The top robot hands now match, or surpass, the dexterity of a human hand; the fastest, strongest and most agile robot bodies already far exceed human capabilities. Machines can be issued verbal instructions and follow them in complex spaces full of obstacles. They are getting ever cheaper to make. The robots are coming.

Yet perhaps not in the way their most boosterish proponents predict. Robots are getting smarter, nimbler and cheaper, but there are still big obstacles to their deployment in lots of commercial settings. Making a robot that can do a given task once is much easier than making one that can do the same task hundreds of times a day for years. Harder still is to build one that can do so in a dusty factory, a greasy kitchen or—God forbid—outside. Initially, robots are likely to do mainly low-value, low-intensity work, perhaps around the home.

The case for boosterism is clear: AI has enormously accelerated the process of teaching robots how to do things. Where state-of-the-art robots were once remote-controlled, or painstakingly manually programmed, they can now be given goals and left to their own devices.

Astra, the latest large-language model (LLM) from OpenAI, is not designed to interact with the physical world. But give it a robot to control and video footage from the robot’s point of view, and that does not seem to matter. If connected to a robotic arm, for instance, Astra can teach itself to paint the Golden Gate Bridge.

I’ll be back

If the recent advances of LLMs can be replicated in the specialist AI models that drive robotics over the next few years, robots will become exponentially more capable. The second a robot system is capable of reliably performing a series of acts of dexterity, it will already have access to the AI smarts required to chain them together into complex tasks, notes Alex Gajewski, co-founder of Pantograph, a robotics lab in California. The result, he argues, will be a robot capable of assembling its own siblings in less than two years. Elon Musk, whose development at Tesla of a humanoid robot called Optimus kick-started a wave of interest in the field, claims that such machines will usher in an age of “amazing abundance” for all.

It is not only the software that is improving; so is the hardware. The consumer drone industry has provided a market for smaller, stronger and more efficient electric motors, which can be adapted to move artificial arms and fingers, notes Ritesh Ragavender, the boss of Reflex, while the improvements in batteries that have propelled electric vehicles are a boon for electric people, too.

The result has been a slashing of the cost of humanoid robots. Visitors to Ludovic Righetti’s robotics lab at New York University are greeted by the skeleton of one of his first such devices. When new, in the mid-2000s, it cost around $1m. Twenty years on, an equivalent machine from a big Chinese manufacturer such as Unitree or Agibot is around $50,000. “The price decrease is insane, and the quality of the robot has increased,” he enthuses. Much of the drop has come in the past two years alone: the average cost of Unitree’s humanoid robots fell 72% between 2023 and 2025, from 593,400 yuan (about $82,600) to 166,400 yuan. Indeed, excitement about Unitree’s advances prompted its shares to jump by more than 400% on their first day of trading in August (see chart).

Scroll through social media and you will find no shortage of awe-inspiring demonstrations of the capabilities of the latest generation of humanoid robots. At the World Humanoid Robot Games in Beijing this summer, for instance, robots competed dazzlingly in sports from high jump to table tennis. Yet performing is in effect the only job such robots do at any scale: Counterpoint, a research firm, says more than 60% of robot shipments in the first half of 2026 were for entertainment, data collection or research.

Robots’ gymnastic feats tell us little about their capabilities, however, owing to what Nvidia’s director of robotics, Jim Fan, calls the “blind gymnast” problem. Robots can be trained much faster in digital simulations than in the real world. Running myriad iterations of the same movement in a virtual space can be done in seconds, for the cost of the computer time. Doing the same thing in the physical world requires access to an actual robot, as well as the “clock time” for it to perform the movements. As a result, robots can rapidly develop capabilities in areas where sim training is a close match for reality, but end up performing the moves like a gymnast carrying out his practised routine, blindfolded. The results are impressive to observe, right up until an unexpected kerb, wall or small child interrupts the performance, at which point the system is unable to adapt and the robot comes crashing down.

To improve things, robot makers are trying three different approaches: improve the simulations, learn from the real world and practise, practise, practise. The first such approach is most appealing for firms with a foot in the world of software: if all robotics’ problems can be solved at the pace it takes to develop new AI models, the field will accelerate sharply.

For some tasks, smarter simulations are a mere programming problem. If your robot trips over a kerb while demonstrating its kung-fu moves, then a virtual training environment with random trip hazards thrown in can improve things. But such an approach is inherently limited. Better to build a training environment that is as messy as the real world by default.

That approach involves building what is known as a “world model”. These systems, in effect an interactive video that simulates an entire scene from the real world, are better at training robots in arbitrary situations. Self-driving cars, including those from Wayve and Waymo, are already trained using world models to help them adapt to outlandish situations. Robotics is following suit.

But world models struggle to simulate situations outside their training data. Eventually, someone needs to actually gather that information from the real world. Asimov, a startup based in San Francisco, pays people to go about their working day festooned in sensors. The company sometimes offers to pay the entire salary of an employee subjected to this treatment, if it considers their work of sufficient interest.

Asimov also runs a cleaning company, which sends a sensor-decked cleaner to conduct an initial three-hour spruce of a customer’s home free of charge. (Subsequent visits start at $10 an hour: repeat data-points, it seems, are less valuable than novel ones.) Tau Robotics, a rival to Asimov, charges customers $60 an hour for cleaning—but brings a humanoid robot to your home to do the job directly.

Your clothes, give them to me

Asimov and Tau are limited by the number of San Franciscans who are comfortable immortalising their greasy crockery and dirty underwear as training data. AgiBot, based in Shanghai, has no such constraint: its 3,000-square-metre “data factory”, which opened in 2024, allows remote-controlled robots to carry out tasks in a series of purpose-built spaces including an entire mock convenience store. “All robotics companies faced the same problem: we have a big lack of data,” says William Shi, who heads Agibot’s European operations. Projects like the data factory help, but in the long run a better solution is scale: the more robots Agibot sells, the more data they gather.

A Californian robotics boss estimates that a fleet of 10,000 robots could gather training data at a much faster clip than YouTube racks up content. Even if a robot’s software is not ready, it can be remotely operated through difficult challenges, learning as it goes. (Such remote operation may, itself, be sufficient to make it economically viable to use robots for certain tasks: some companies use workers in low-wage countries to oversee small fleets of semi-autonomous robotic delivery vehicles, for instance.)

Copious data is of little use, however, if robots are not physically capable of a given job. Building them is an exercise in trade-offs. Some are familiar from other industries: a larger battery makes a robot heavier, which in turn runs the battery down; a more dexterous hand is more expensive, which may be overkill if the task can be completed with simple pincers.

Others are more specific to robotics: a device which is strong enough to carry out arduous physical labour probably poses a safety risk to squishy human bodies nearby. Unlike the race to build ever-better software, robotics companies are trying to work out exactly where the boundaries of “good enough” lie.

Some goals remain distant. Although robotic hands can be extremely dexterous, they cannot match human ones for sensitivity, Dr Righetti says. It is trivially easy for people to find a single grain of sand by touch or to discern even quite small differences in the temperature of objects they pick up. Building such capabilities into a robot would not just be an engineering challenge, but also a programming one.

Instead, most hardware companies are focused on mundane capabilities. Wires that are bent over and over again will eventually break. Reflex wants those in its robots to withstand 20m bends at a minimum. As high as that sounds, a robot working 24/7 will reach that threshold in just four years with any motion it undertakes ten times a minute. Mr Shi of AgiBot demurs when asked how long its flagship A3 robot, an adult-sized machine that retails for more than $70,000, can last before something breaks, but noted that it comes with an 18-month warranty.

When reliability gets good enough, the next challenge is scale. More than four in five of the world’s humanoid robots are in China. Reuben Scriven, an analyst, predicts it will retain that lead until at least 2035, since only Chinese firms are currently embarking on mass production. Some non-Chinese robotics firms, such as Figure and 1x, have raised heady sums, but the cash has mostly been ploughed into research rather than manufacturing.

The result, according to John VerWey of the Centre for Security and Emerging Technology, a think-tank, is that Western robotics suppliers have been cautious about expanding. “Component suppliers are pretty conservative firms run in Germany and Japan that serve relatively boring end-use markets,” Mr VerWey notes. “They run a tight ship.” Firms used to serving staid industries such as carmaking and health care are loth to expand “far more than they’ve ever historically known”. Almost every humanoid uses custom components, Mr Scriven adds, which will not get cheaper without a sturdier supply chain.

Safety is another conundrum. Making devices safe enough to interact with humans can reduce their productivity, Mr Scriven notes. Robots that work alongside humans in factories operate at a slower pace than those that are sequestered in cages. Software improvements may one day obviate the need for such precautions, but the regulatory standards to permit that do not yet exist.

That leaves robotics firms labouring to soften the trade-offs. Lighter materials help: 1x, which was founded in Norway, clads its adult-sized machines in a fabric covering resembling Nordic knitwear, and makes the tendons in its robots’ hands out of special polymers to reduce the weight further. Fauna, a robotics firm based in New York, is going further still with Sprout, a metre-high, soft, lightweight robot designed for home use. Its software prioritises safety, too, says Rob Cochran, Fauna’s boss: if you push or pull Sprout’s arms, it yields quickly. It is programmed to be gentle in a way most robots are not.

You’re terminated

Other firms turn concern about safety on its head. Threehalves, a centaur-shaped robot from Satyress, a startup in California, is designed to do jobs that are dangerous for humans. It can handle a chainsaw, for instance, but is designed for only limited interaction with people. Its goat-like head and horns are not just intimidating, but also stop it fitting through a normal-sized doorway. If that doesn’t work, the company advertises a handy fail-safe: simply stab or shoot it in the chest. The resulting breach of its pneumatics system will “render the robot immobile”.

Threehalves also embodies (literally) the debate about what shape general-purpose robots should take. Although humanoids predominate, some question whether that form makes sense. Anthony Milovantsev, an analyst, says that firms like the idea of staffing factories and warehouses with devices capable of many tasks, but see no need for them to resemble humans. Efficiency at picking, packing and unloading are the main objectives.

That makes sense. Technically demanding, relatively unstable and expensive legs are probably a liability on robots destined for hard labour. Devices intended for the home, however, are more likely to take a reassuring humanoid form.

Mr Ragavender, the boss of Reflex, explains that its devices are intended for use in industrial settings which are typically step-free. His robots’ wheeled bases are more stable, stronger and therefore safer than legs. But their top half remains humanoid, because to be otherwise would undermine their general-purpose billing.

Robots with vaguely human shapes have a big advantage in that they can learn from how real humans perform tasks and eventually replace them with minimal adjustment. It is simply a matter of replicating the motions. But humanoid form is not just about mechanics, argues Mr Cochran of Fauna; it is also about the relationship between the robot and the humans around it. Sprout, Fauna’s robot, has expressive eyebrows, gesticulates as it talks and even beeps and boops in ways that are, Mr Cochran says, “engaging and meaningful” (he means cute). “Robots won’t be perfect from day one, so how do you build something that has an emotional connection?”

Many analysts see the home as the natural proving ground for humanoid robots. Morgan Stanley, a bank, predicts there could be 1.6m of them in American homes by 2040, at about $30,000 a pop. Keerthana Gopalakrishnan of Google DeepMind is more bullish: she foresees 1m robots in homes across America by 2031.

Home use makes a virtue of robots’ current weaknesses. A useful domestic dogsbody does not need huge strength, which makes safety simpler. Enthusiastic early adopters may well pay more to own a live-in robot than it would cost to hire humans to do the same work, helping robotics firms to scale and so descend the cost curve. (Even so, Mr Milovantsev’s research suggests that most Americans would not pay more than $5,000 for such a machine, much less than the $20,000 that 1x charges.) Best of all, home use is less intensive than industrial work, so robots will have more time to justify the initial outlay before needing to be repaired or replaced.

Domestic robots may even generate less “societal friction” than industrial ones, to use a euphemism coined by Per Roman, an investment banker. A robot that takes the housework off your hands is easy to cast as a force for good. One that takes your job is an obvious villain.

Meanwhile, Robocurve, an evaluation firm, estimates that frontier LLMs’ capacity to perform real-world tasks with robots is doubling every 54 days. Such systems will soon overtake specialist robotics models, it believes. The robots are on the march. ■
