Why there are so many bizarre national days

National Hermit Day, anyone? Or National Button Day?

FOR AMERICANS, at least, November is the month of Thanksgiving. Each year, on the fourth Thursday, Americans are encouraged to feel grateful as they gather with family and gobble up 46m turkeys. Yet there are other, odder things that calendars suggest people should be thankful for in November. There is National Butter Day, Bison Day, Seatbelt Day and Button Day. (Forget zips and laces and celebrate “the function of buttons”!) If that all sounds a bit absurd, well, there is also National Absurdity Day on November 20th.

There are more such national “holidays” than there are days in the year. They span the very serious (National Sanctity of Human Life Day) and the very silly (National Pretend to be a Time Traveller Day). There are scheduling conflicts, as when the day of Remembrance for Road Traffic Victims coincides with Raisin Bran Cereal Day. Many have uninspiring names (National Carbonated Beverage with Caffeine Day), or lack inspiration altogether (National Eat a Cranberry Day falls just after National Cranberry Relish Day). Who is anointing all these days—and why?

Proper national celebrations are approved by governments. The last time a federal holiday was made in America was in 2021, when Joe Biden formalised Juneteenth to commemorate the end of slavery. The United Nations designates global celebrations, such as International Women’s Day, to publicise causes. As you might expect, goofy days are easier to create. There are self-appointed day-makers, such as National Day Calendar, which acts like “Wikipedia for national days”, according to its founder, Marlo Anderson.

It is free to submit a day to National Day Calendar, and anyone can do it. The company gets more than 30,000 applications a year, says Mr Anderson, and there is a “very tough” selection process so only a handful are chosen. (You can’t help but wonder, then, how National Barcode Day got through.) New days are often suggested by charities, or companies seeking to boost sales. Though they are called “national” days, they are promoted internationally: National Day Calendar helps brands advertise relevant days through their website, social channels and media such as television and newspapers.

National days trend online. People sometimes post more about #PizzaDay or #AvocadoDay than about breaking news events. Yet such odd holidays predate the internet. In 1909 raisin-growers in California created National Raisin Day, sharing discounts and recipes to mark the occasion. When Father’s Day emerged in the early 20th century people complained it was a frivolous gimmick.

Yet today other family-oriented days, such as National Siblings Day or National Daughter Day, are also popular. They certainly seem easier to celebrate than some of the more niche occasions. Dan Brotzel, a journalist, tried observing a silly holiday every day for a year. On National Hermit Day he interviewed a Catholic hermit (naturally, she had no idea the day existed); on National Cabbage Day he stared “very intently at cabbage for a while”. It can be hard to decide whether such celebrations are a harmless bit of fun or totally foolish. Why not wait until Make Up Your Mind Day in December? ■
