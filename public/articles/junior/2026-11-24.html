The first private-sector space station is due to go into orbit

Lots of others also think they see a gap in the market

By Geoffrey Carr, Senior editor, Science & technology, The Economist

When a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket launches Haven-1 in 2027, it will be the first privately built and operated space station. It is the brainchild of Jed McCaleb, a tech entrepreneur who set up a firm called Vast Space in 2021 to build it. The station is an aluminium cylinder, covered in shielding to stop it being punctured by micrometeorites and space debris. It is 10.1 metres long and 4.4 metres in diameter, with a pressurised volume of 80 cubic metres, a docking port at one end for the SpaceX taxi service that will carry people to and from it, and an observation window at the other. It can accommodate up to four people. Once it is in orbit, four missions are planned, each lasting two weeks.

The price of a stay is not yet public information, but Vast hopes to attract government customers (it has already agreed to host two French astronauts) and private firms interested in microgravity research. The real prize, though, will be the knowledge needed to build—starting in 2028—a multi-module, permanently crewed version, Haven Station. This, the firm hopes, may be chosen by NASA, America’s space agency, as a private-sector alternative to its lodgings on board the ageing International Space Station (ISS), which is due to be scrapped in the 2030s.

Vast is not alone in such hopes. Three other space-station startups, also vying for NASA’s custom, aim to go straight to the permanently crewed stage. The most advanced are those proposed by Axiom Space (like Vast, founded for the purpose of building such a station) and Starlab (a collaboration between Voyager Technologies, an American firm, and Airbus, a European one).

Axiom’s offering, Axiom Station, will, like Haven Station, be assembled from multiple modules. A service module, to be launched in 2028, will start off as an appendage of the ISS before detaching to join the habitable part, Hab-1, which will go up in 2029. Starlab, also pencilled in for 2029, will be a single cylinder, like Haven-1. But it will have five times the pressurised volume, and will require SpaceX’s next-generation Starship to carry it into orbit.

Residents of these stations will enjoy all mod cons. Axiom hired Philippe Starck, a French designer, to help with the lighting on Hab-1. Starlab has plumped for Journey, a design agency, and Hilton, a hotel chain, to make things nice and cosy.

Trailing the field is Orbital Reef, a design based on inflatable modules proposed by Sierra Space, a firm in Denver that is also working on a spaceplane, and Blue Origin, Jeff Bezos’s space company. It would be launched on Blue Origin’s New Glenn heavy-lift rocket.

As for the ISS, its proposed fate is a controlled splashdown at Point Nemo, the spot in the Pacific Ocean farthest from human habitation. But the ISS has outlived three previous attempts to kill it off, and may well do so again. ■
