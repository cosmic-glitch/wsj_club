The Climate Issue newsletter: The world braces for an almighty El Niño

Rachel Dobbs, our news editor, explains why the worst is yet to come

The governments of Bolivia, Panama and Peru have declared states of emergency. In the Indian state of Telangana, a lack of water led the government to ask farmers not to cultivate water-intensive rice this winter, comparing the crisis to the covid-19 pandemic. The Intergovernmental Authority on Development, an East African trade bloc, is urging its members to step up surveillance and get vaccines ready as the risk of a Rift Valley Fever outbreak rises.

These are just some of the actions being taken in the face of the largest El Niño event on record. El Niños are the warm phase of the great climatic seesaw known as ENSO. Two to four times a decade, the hottest waters of the Pacific spread from west to east, which puts more heat into the atmosphere and changes weather patterns around the tropics and beyond. The current whopper, which began in June, is expected to peak around December

Already, the eastern Pacific has seen record hurricane activity, and the North Atlantic almost none—typical El Niño effects. Indonesia has been hit by drought and the terrible wildfires that come with it. Coastal towns in California have been flooded—as have their counterparts south of the equator. In October the port city of Guayaquil, in Ecuador, saw half a metre of rain, about 100 times the norm. “Peru and Ecuador are bracing for the mother of all modern [El Niño] events,” said Abraham Levy, a Peruvian meteorologist.

Bracing, because the worst is yet to come. El Niños’ peaks are defined by how far the sea-surface temperatures have risen above average in a particular bit of the tropical Pacific known as Niño 3.4. They will not reach their highest point until the end of this month or the beginning of December. And many of the effects lag behind the upset that is driving them

Forecasters expect drought in South-East Asia, Australia and southern Africa, and floods in east Africa, and the southern United States. El Salvador and Honduras have issued red alerts for drought. The UN’s World Food Programme reckons the effects could push an extra 49m people into hunger globally.

What marks this El Niño out as a particular concern is that it is hitting a planet already forced well beyond the temperatures it is used to. It is also the largest such event ever seen. In the El Niño of 2015 (which was itself unusually powerful) the Niño 3.4 temperature anomaly peaked at 3.02°C. This year’s swept past that record in September with a good two months to go. Forecasts from 14 models put the peak at around 4°C: more than a degree above the record. The anomaly is so big that Europe’s main weather forecaster was forced to extend the y-axis on one of the charts it uses to track it.

“This El Niño event is developing so quickly and is reaching sea surface temperatures so warm that it’s simply staggering,” Zeke Hausfather, a climate scientist, recently wrote in Carbon Brief. “We haven’t seen anything like this in the historical record and it may end up being the strongest El Niño event in the past millennium.” Tree rings from places where rainfall responds to El Niño across the equatorial Pacific suggest no winter anomaly before 1871 topped about 2.1°C; this winter’s is forecast at 3.9°C.

El Niño is a natural phenomenon, and proxy records show it has always waxed and waned. But natural factors alone are very unlikely to produce an anomaly as big as this one; James Hansen, a former NASA scientist, argued that this event’s abnormal strength is itself “likely human-driven”. Either way, it is being driven by an ocean which, thanks to human activities, absorbed a record amount of heat last year

That makes it a test not just of forecasters, but of everyone downstream of them. Rupert Read, a British philosopher and campaigner, pointed out that many countries keep strategic oil reserves, but almost none hold reserves of fertiliser—much of which, until recently, came through the Strait of Hormuz. Coping with climate change will mean adapting to conditions humans have never experienced. This El Niño will be one of the first big tests.
