The strange satisfaction of eating on a plane

Crackers and vacuum-packed cheese, sir?

You are handed a small printed menu by a member of the airline crew. You examine it with interest, even though you know full well that the only options will be chicken or pasta. Each item has a description that is at once accurate and highly deceptive. Seasonal salad with olive-oil vinaigrette; herb-encrusted chicken on a bed of rice with a tomato jus; Basque cheesecake; crackers and cheddar cheese. You are well aware what these words mean in practice—the salad will be a lettuce leaf and a sachet, the cheese will be vacuum-packed—but you can’t help feeling the smallest ripple of excitement.

Why should this be? Eating airline food has almost nothing to recommend it, and yet there is something strangely satisfying about the experience. Is it the lack of things to do that makes any activity exciting? Is it the comfort of participating in one of life’s most ritualised experiences? Is the lack of oxygen affecting your critical faculties?

The arrival of the aperitif supplies an answer. You are getting a free drink and the world’s tiniest bag of pretzels from someone who calls you “sir” or “madam”. What’s not to like?

Soon enough the meal itself arrives. “Will you be dining with us?” asks a crew member, absurdly. “No, I thought I’d try the new Italian place instead,” you say, and are rewarded with a wintry smile. “Can I have the chicken?” you say.

In the most expensive seats, you might be served your meal on a plate. (Of all the tricks pulled off by the aviation industry, this is the most remarkable: in what other circumstances would eating food off a plate be regarded as a luxury?) Back in the cheap seats, a tray is deposited on the table in front of you. It is a fiesta of plastic. It is also tightly packed, a sliding-block puzzle of little dishes which, once moved, can never be made to fit snugly again.

You are asked if you would like some wine. Of course you would. It’s eight in the morning but the wine is free. Your neighbour asks if he could have two bottles, and you wish you had thought of that.

You unroll your napkin, and immediately drop your fork somewhere in the darkness below you. There’s no chance of retrieving it. You take one very large bread roll and try to apply your very small portion of butter to it. It’s a maths problem to which there is no good answer. Claude could solve it, but not you. You chew and chew, and use the wine to soften things up in your mouth. Delicious.

Now for the salad. You take off the plastic lid, which has the same properties as the overall ensemble: once removed, it cannot be put back on again. You gingerly balance the salad lid on top of another dish, and improvise with a knife and spoon to scoop up the lettuce leaf. A casual observer would assume this was your first encounter with cutlery.

Next you carefully remove the foil from your chicken dish, and put that in the empty salad dish. You saw at the chicken with a plastic knife. It gives way at the pace of coastal erosion.

While you are whittling away at the chicken, your neighbour has ordered more wine. An array of bottles perches on the edge of his tray. He tips one of them over by accident, and in attempting to catch it, slams his hand down on his tray. A piece of Basque cheesecake rises majestically into the air and vaults two rows forward. There is a small cry of surprise from a fellow passenger. She knew that things could fall out of the overhead lockers, but no one warned her about a hail of cheesecake. Your neighbour seems not to have noticed the loss of his dessert and is now drinking straight from the bottle.

You eventually finish dissecting your chicken, which has not exactly taxed the taste buds but was very hot, and turn your attention to your own cheesecake. You take yet another lid off. Various pieces of plastic teeter on top of each other. Your tray resembles something that Alexander Calder might have made.

To finish, you turn your attention to a rectangle of orange cheese. It’s impossible to prise it out of its plastic wrapper by hand, so you use your teeth. Even as you shake your head from side to side to tear the packaging open, you feel you are being treated well. You place the cheese rectangle between two dry crackers, and bite into it. Crumbs spray you and your neighbour, though he is now snoring loudly and beyond caring. You take a final sip of wine to help you swallow. You carefully hand back the tray to a member of the crew. Only a few hours more, you think, and you can enjoy a light breakfast before landing. ■
