The oddball who founded UPS—and changed the world

James Casey’s obsession with efficiency helped make next-day delivery possible

The UPS Man: James E. Casey and the Creation of Modern Logistics. By Marc Levinson. NYU Press; 336 pages; $30 and £23.99

JAMES CASEY is far from an easy subject for a biographer. Unlike many modern business titans, the founder of the United Parcel Service (UPS) was reclusive. He wrote no memoirs and left behind no letters when he died, aged 95, in 1983. He seems to have had no interests other than his work: he spent his Sundays driving delivery routes in his Packard, comparing his times with those expected of drivers. The New Yorker, in a rare profile, said that “he talks only about parcels.”

Anyone writing about Casey, then, must do two things: doggedly hunt for information and make a dull person’s life interesting. Marc Levinson, a historian, economist and former editor at this newspaper, succeeds on both counts. Just as his previous book, “The Box”, used the shipping container to tell the story of globalisation, this one explores how Casey changed the world of commerce.

Today, people in the rich world expect that anything they buy online will show up on their doorsteps the next day. “More than 300,000 packages are delivered somewhere in the world every minute,” Mr Levinson observes. Casey built a fledgling messenger service into a Fortune 500 firm, and changed how people shop. (If you have ever successfully ordered a Christmas gift at the last minute, you have him to thank.)

In 1907 Casey’s first business was one of many in Seattle, his hometown, that delivered packages from department stores to buyers’ houses. With messengers taking each item in turn from shop to customer, and each short trip fetching a few cents, it was difficult to turn a profit. So Casey decided to consolidate. He had retailers send all their outgoing items to a central location, where they would be sorted by destination. This made delivery routes more efficient; it also allowed him to charge a premium for a same-day delivery service.

He offered retailers long-term contracts, which saved them from having to spend money on operations (which, in the early 20th century, meant horses and wagons). For Casey, it guaranteed volume and financial surety; and made it harder for rivals to poach his customers.

He was relentless in seeking better ways to work. In the 1920s Casey hired an engineer who had worked at General Motors and Ford to design machinery that would help sort parcels. Trucks were loaded in reverse order of delivery, so that drivers wasted no time scrabbling around for the right item. UPS sent observers armed with stopwatches on deliveries in order to calculate an “ideal performance” for each trip. That benchmark was then used to award bonuses to drivers who made more deliveries than expected. Similar methods are now commonplace at companies such as Amazon. But it was Casey who first embraced automation and an “unapologetic insistence on efficient procedures”.

And competitors “were to be harassed or simply eradicated”. UPS hounded challengers by filing umpteen “complaints, protests and objections”. It flouted antitrust laws by secretly buying up rivals, locking retailers into exclusive contracts and striking deals to carve up markets with competitors. It also persuaded the authorities that “regulations should…be tailored to its needs alone,” winning exemptions from rules that constrained other firms.

In that regard, Casey was again ahead of his time. He was a “demanding taskmaster” with a ruthless, “win-at-all-costs” mentality—the kind that has become de rigueur in Silicon Valley and beyond. The lesson from this book, budding entrepreneurs may feel, is that you should stop at nothing to deliver the goods. ■
