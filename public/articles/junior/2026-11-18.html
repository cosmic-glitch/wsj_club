New long-haul flights will connect unlikely cities in 2027

Anyone need to fly from Philadelphia to Porto direct?

By Leo Mirani, Ashoka columnist, The Economist

At some point in October 2027 an Airbus A350 will take off from Sydney with 238 passengers on board and spend as many as 22 hours in the air, depending on the route and winds. When it lands in London almost a full day later, it will have set a new record for the world’s longest scheduled commercial flight, currently held by a Singapore Airlines service from Singapore to New York (which takes a measly 19 hours).

Qantas, Australia’s flag-carrier and the operator of the marathon flight, has ordered a fleet of 12 specially customised, “ultra-long-range” versions of the A350-1000. It did not do that just to gain entry into the record books. The airline reckons there is enough demand to sustain daily nonstop flights between Sydney and London. It will follow up with a daily nonstop Sydney-New York service. And unlike the Singapore Airlines flight, which uses a cabin configured with only premium seating, Qantas offers the full range—from economy seats at the back all the way to a first-class experience that it says “blurs the lines between air travel and boutique accommodation”. (The lucky folk in the cheap seats will have access to a “well-being zone” with a “hydration station”—aka, a place to stretch and drink water.)

A plane that can fly from Sydney to London is, in effect, capable of flying between any two points on Earth. Yet the Qantas service is unlikely to spur a spate of new routes from Tokyo to Tulum, or Panama to Manama. Rather than a glorious new era for ultra-long-haul flights, the Sydney-London and Sydney-New York services will mark their culmination.

One reason is that the longer an aircraft flies nonstop, the more fuel it needs to carry, including fuel to carry the weight of all the extra fuel. A 20-hour flight thus needs more of the stuff than two ten-hour trips. That limits ultra-long routes to those with strong demand and, more important, lots of well-to-do travellers willing to shell out for premium cabins.

There are only a handful of such routes. City pairs with lots of migrants, family-and-friends visitors, and tourists in both directions, such as London-Sydney, are one type. A second is made up of busy commercial corridors such as between San Francisco and Bangalore. And a third is composed of connections between hubs, such as Doha-Los Angeles or Hong Kong-New York. Think of these not as flights between two cities but between two regions. Most passengers will be heading to or from another city—or both.

Yet just because the world is running out of very distant places to put within reach of a single hop does not mean it is running out of new connections altogether. In 2024 the first Airbus A321XLR—which stands for “Xtra Long Range”—entered service with Iberia, Spain’s flag-carrier, which uses it to connect Boston and Madrid. The single-aisle plane, a variant of the type usually used for short hops, can spend up to 11 hours in the air and is perfect for what the aviation industry calls a “long and thin” route: “long” referring to distance and “thin” to demand. The appeal of the XLR lies in its ability to fly long distances without the costs of a big plane like a Boeing 777 or Airbus A350, thereby opening up routes which would otherwise have been unprofitable.

After years of delays and supply-chain disruptions across the aviation industry, deliveries of A321XLRs picked up in 2026 and will accelerate in 2027. American Airlines will start flights between Philadelphia and Porto, Portugal’s second city. IndiGo, India’s biggest airline, already uses the plane to fly nonstop from Mumbai to Amsterdam. Expect many more “long and thin” routes in 2027. Just as airlines like Ryanair made it common to fly point-to-point within Europe, aircraft like the A321XLR make it possible to do so across oceans and continents. The era of ultra-long-haul may have peaked. But the age of “long and thin” is only just starting. ■
