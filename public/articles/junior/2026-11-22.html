China’s Buddhists are releasing animals where they do not belong

It is ruffling feathers in the Communist Party

On a drizzly Saturday night this month, someone released dozens of exotic birds near the ancient Longhua Temple in Shanghai. Alas, the city gets chilly at this time of year: tiger parrots and zebra finches prefer the tropics. Most quickly died from the cold, or at the claws of cats. The temple said it did not know who freed the birds. But it was probably a Buddhist conducting a ritual known as fang sheng. Some believers reckon that giving captive animals their freedom is a way to earn spiritual merit. The increasingly popular practice, however, is worrying officials and environmentalists.

During Mao’s Cultural Revolution, the enthusiastically atheist Communist Party banned fang sheng as a “feudal superstition” (and, in hard times, a waste of protein). Officials filled in temple ponds that were once reserved for freed fish. Since Mao’s death in 1976, Buddhism has seen a revival. Relatively few people formally identify with the religion. But surveys suggest that perhaps a third of Chinese people engage in some of the practices associated with it, from burning incense at temples to fang sheng. China’s abundance of markets selling live animals for the dinner table makes it easy for the devout, with money, to acquire religious merit.

In the past most fang sheng ceremonies might have just involved saving a fish or two from the chopping block and slipping them into a nearby river. But in recent decades believers have become both wealthier and more inventive. Some focus on quantity: last year around 400 cats were released near a reservoir in southern China (several drowned); fish are sometimes poured into waterways by the thousand, to the delight of local anglers. Others go for quality, in the form of exotic species (in 2012 a South American piranha turned up in a Chinese river). Technology is enabling new ways of performing fang sheng. Earlier this year a fang sheng ceremony was live-streamed from a riverbank in south-west China; those watching online could earn merit by sending money to the streamer, who would then take a baby turtle from a box and pop it in the water.

All this fuels illegal trade in wildlife. Many animals are captured or bred simply to be sold to Buddhists. Bird traders, for instance, set up shop outside temples to attract the sympathy of worshippers. And foreign species, if they do not perish, can also wreak havoc on China’s ecosystems. One of the worst offenders is the red-eared slider turtle, a native of North America that can outbreed local reptiles and spread infections. Beijing’s canals are infested with them.

Over the past decade officials have been trying harder to protect the environment. This has included efforts to regulate fang sheng more tightly. Since 2021 releasing invasive species has been a criminal offence. To avoid hefty fines, people are now supposed only to free approved species in designated spots, and register releases in advance with the government. In 2023 a woman in the eastern province of Jiangsu was fined some 90,000 yuan ($13,500) after dumping 12.5 tonnes of catfish in a lake. The state-affiliated Buddhist Association of China has denounced those who profit from fang sheng and has called for the practice to become more “scientific and reasonable”. Public opinion may be shifting, too. The release of the unlucky birds in Shanghai was greeted by squawks of disapproval on social media.

Still, investigations by state media suggest few people are telling the government about their activities (“I’m doing a good deed, why should I have to report it?” complained one Buddhist to reporters). And the practice is hard to police. Mr Gao, a 35-year-old in Shanghai who works in finance, has been releasing fish regularly for years, but has never sought government permission. At first he had hoped that fang sheng might make his work go more smoothly. Then he started to enjoy simply watching the fish swim away. He only releases a few at a time, and picks local species. Mr Gao admits that not every Buddhist is so careful. “It is easy to have good intentions but do bad things,” he says. ■
