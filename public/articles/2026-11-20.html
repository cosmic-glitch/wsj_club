What is causing America’s recent outages

Frenzied preparations for cyber-attacks are creating their own risks

“Once is happenstance. Twice is coincidence,” observes Goldfinger, the eponymous villain of Ian Fleming’s novel, to James Bond. “The third time it’s enemy action.” That has been the conclusion of many Americans, too, as they watch the digital world seemingly crumble around them in recent days. On November 12th Microsoft services experienced a global outage, leaving office workers adrift. The next day a glitch in air-traffic control communications caused the longest flight stoppage since the attacks on September 11th, 2001. On November 16th technical problems afflicted several big banks. And on November 17th the National Security Agency (NSA), America’s signals-intelligence service, was hit by disruption to its own classified communication system.

The explanations from America’s officials have not inspired confidence. Markwayne Mullin, Donald Trump’s homeland security secretary, suggested on November 16th that the spate of outages might have been caused by “self-fixing” AI that was rewriting its own code too quickly, a theory he had gleaned from speaking to “one guy that’s very smart with tech”. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) said that a faulty router was to blame for the problems with air-traffic control. Josh Hawley, a Republican senator, is among the Americans who don’t buy it. “The full Senate ought to be briefed on what exactly is going on,” he demanded on November 17th. “If this is all a coincidence, it’s like the greatest coincidence in the history of the world.”

America’s week of digital mayhem is indeed unusual. But it is not necessarily suspicious. There is little evidence that malicious hackers or wayward AI are to blame. Rather, some of the recent chaos could be a symptom of efforts to make America’s digital infrastructure more secure. Tinkering with such old and complex systems has always been fraught. But companies are now using AI to discover vulnerabilities and moving more quickly to repair them. Paradoxically, that defensive activity seems to be creating new short-term risks.

On the face of it, each of this month’s mishaps has a prosaic explanation. The FAA says its initial investigation found that a new router installed at a radio communications site in El Dorado, Arkansas, led to problems at a radar site in Amarillo, Texas, flooding the system with “excessive data”. The work was part of routine upgrades from old copper wires to fibre-optic cables, rather than anything to do with newfangled AI systems. Sean Duffy, the transport secretary, demanded that Congress pony up $50bn for upgrades.

In Microsoft’s case, its issues stemmed from a patch to software rolled out in July. The patch had worked for four months, but contained a hidden bug that only cropped up later when data from two different systems inadvertently piled up on one network at the same time as server capacity was down. The result was a cascading failure, causing more than 80% of servers to keel over. Notably, JPMorganChase, a big bank, and NSA sources both say that the problems with their systems were also linked to software updates.

“I don’t think the events themselves are connected in any real meaningful or material way,” argues Chris Krebs, who led America’s Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency from 2018 to 2020. But there are commonalities. Each of these cases involves an effort to make a system more secure. The outages at Microsoft, the big banks and the NSA might have an additional link, to AI, though not for the reasons given by Mr Mullin.

Mark Warner, the vice-chair of the Senate Intelligence Committee, offered a more thorough explanation on November 17th. “AI is making it possible to identify vulnerabilities across enormous numbers of systems at unprecedented speed,” he wrote on X, “creating enormous pressure on companies and government agencies to patch those vulnerabilities immediately, often all at once, putting tremendous strain on systems that weren’t built to handle that pace of change.”

In April Anthropic, an AI firm, announced Mythos, a model which turned out to be extremely good at discovering and exploiting vulnerabilities in software. Firms and organisations granted special access to Mythos through a programme known as Glasswing, and have been using it to “tear down their existing code to discover vulnerabilities and patch them before bad guys can use their own tools to find them”, says Mr Krebs.

The sheer “volume and velocity” of that effort can cause issues, he notes, overwhelming the teams that deal with vulnerabilities and patches. Microsoft is an example of this. Between 2022 and 2025 the firm fixed about 4,000 “common vulnerabilities and exposures”, or CVEs in cyber-parlance, a security flaw in software that is catalogued with a unique number. In 2026 alone—assisted by Mythos and other models—the firm published more than 5,000, a staggering tally that is still growing (see chart). Ciaran Martin, who ran Britain’s cyber-security agency from 2016 to 2020, says it is plausible, though not yet proven, that AI-related layoffs might also have “stripped organisations bare of people who understand how their networks work”. That would make mistakes more likely.

In the short term, the result looks like digital bedlam. “Every incident…conjures fears of a cyber-attack,” observes Colin Clarke of the Soufan Centre, an intelligence and security research group. “Iran, Russia, and China live in Americans’ heads rent-free.” Those anxieties are compounded by Americans’ lack of trust in their government, he adds. It doesn’t help that real cyber-attacks continue. In October hackers linked to North Korea stole a whopping $6.3bn from Binance, a crypto firm.

In the long term, the process of frenetic AI-driven patching is good news, argues Mr Krebs. Many vulnerabilities will have been “swept out” in the coming months and years, he says, resulting in code that is “safer and more secure”. But Americans may take scant comfort in the prospect of more outages now and ever-more sophisticated attacks later.■
